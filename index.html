01 / STORY

My story

From humanities and self-study to building operational tools.

I spent three years preparing for India’s civil services exam. I took it up because I wanted to study the humanities, and I chose psychology as my optional subject. After each failed attempt I went back, worked out what I was missing and rebuilt my method. It was hard, and often lonely, and it taught me two things: how to keep going when the odds are long, and how to look at a problem from several angles before taking a side.

Psychology stayed with me. I like working out why people do what they do, and I notice quickly when something is off in a team, a tool or a screen.

Watch how people actually do a job, find the root cause of what slows them down, then build something that makes the right way the easy way.

Engineering is how I act on that. My best work follows that pattern.

Before all of this, I played baseball in college. It was play for me, not work. I cared less about the result than about getting a little better every game, and I still work that way.

What I’ve built

Production tools used by real teams today.

IT support diagnostics

Ticketing and automated root-cause analysis
In use today

IT support depended on engineers diagnosing each problem from memory and typing fixes by hand, machine by machine. I built a ticketing system with a diagnostic engine. It reads signals from the user’s PC, works out the likely root cause, and fixes the low-risk problems by itself, behind safety checks and with the ability to undo.

C# .NET 8 WPF MySQL YAML rules
The approach and what it’s built with
  • Collectors read common health signals from a PC, such as processor, memory, disk, network, printing and sign-in.
  • A rule engine turns those signals into findings across a few dozen root-cause categories, each with a confidence score. The rules live in an editable file, so the logic can change without rewriting the software.
  • A small set of low-risk fixes can run on their own, but only when three checks pass: a master switch is on, the fix is classed as low risk, and it is on an approved list. The previous state is saved so a failed fix can be rolled back.
  • End users get plain-English steps. IT agents get the technical detail.
  • Includes SLA tracking, role-based access and an audit trail. The installer was cut from about 224 MB to about 33 MB.
  • I built it with AI coding agents. I made the design decisions, including the safety rules, and reviewed the output.

Inventory valuation

Month-end accounting automation for a manufacturer
In use today

A manufacturer valued its inventory using many spreadsheets and a long manual checklist. I built a pipeline that calculates the valuation to the international inventory standard (IAS 2) and produces an audit-ready Excel file. A finance team member can trace any number back to its source.

Python MySQL openpyxl Tkinter
The approach and what it’s built with
  • For each product it works out the cost and the net realisable value, then applies the lower-of-the-two rule that the standard requires.
  • The cost build-up combines material rates, production costs and allocated overheads.
  • Money is calculated with exact decimal arithmetic instead of floating-point numbers. The Excel output keeps the original column references, so auditors can compare it with the old workbook.
  • A formula-by-formula guide maps every calculation to its source data and code, so someone other than me can maintain it.
  • It has a desktop interface because the web version could not be installed on the users’ machines.
  • I built it with AI coding agents. I set the rules and checked the results.

Mindset, habits, and how I fit into a team.

Play is when the work is its own reward. In college baseball I wasn’t worried about results. I was absorbed in the game and tried to get a little better each time. I look for the same thing at work: problems I’m curious enough about to keep going without being pushed.

What experience taught me

  • Finding root causes. I trace a symptom back to what is really behind it, in code, in tools and in how people work.
  • Making sense of a lot of information. Three years of exam preparation taught me to take a huge, messy body of material and give it a clear structure.
  • Weighing a problem from several sides. I try to stay neutral and look at the evidence before choosing a position.
  • Reading people. Studying psychology made me better at noticing when someone is struggling or a process isn’t working for them.
  • Teaching myself. I learned governance, psychology and now building with AI agents without a classroom.
  • Explaining things plainly. I write documentation and guides so that the knowledge doesn’t live in one person’s head.

How I can contribute

  • I work between the people who do the job and the people who build the tools, and I help each side understand the other.
  • I turn a vague problem into a clear statement of what needs fixing, then build a first working version myself.
  • I’m comfortable across areas, such as IT, finance and operations, and I learn a new area quickly.
  • I write things down, so the team can keep using and improving what I build after I’ve moved on.
